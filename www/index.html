ARTEMIS

NASA's Artemis program is taking people back to the Moon – and this time, to stay.
Here is the timeline as of November 2026.

✔ Completed · November 2022

Artemis I

  • An uncrewed test flight around the Moon.
  • The first flight of the SLS rocket and the Orion spacecraft together.
✔ Completed · April 1–10, 2026

Artemis II

  • Four astronauts flew around the Moon on a trip of about 10 days – the first crewed flight to the Moon since 1972.
  • The crew: Reid Wiseman (commander), Victor Glover (pilot), Christina Koch and Jeremy Hansen of Canada.
  • They traveled farther from Earth than any humans before them, breaking Apollo 13's distance record.
  • Their spacecraft, named Integrity, splashed down in the Pacific Ocean off San Diego.
Planned for 2027

Artemis III

  • A practice mission in orbit around the Earth – like Apollo 9 before the Apollo 11 landing.
  • Orion will dock with test versions of both Moon landers: Blue Origin's Blue Moon and SpaceX's Starship.
  • The crew, announced in June 2026: Randy Bresnik (commander), Luca Parmitano of Italy (pilot, European Space Agency), Frank Rubio and Andre Douglas.
  • The astronauts will also test the new spacesuits made for walking on the Moon.
Planned for early 2028

Artemis IV

  • The first crewed Moon landing since Apollo 17 in 1972.
  • The target: the Moon's south pole region, where shadowed craters may hold water ice.
  • Starship will dock with Orion in orbit around the Earth and push both of them to the Moon.
Planned for late 2028

Artemis V

  • A second Moon landing, in the same year as Artemis IV.
  • The start of building a permanent outpost on the Moon.
  • Before the crews arrive, a heavy cargo delivery is planned to land large equipment at the south pole.
  • The long-term goal: use what we learn on the Moon to reach Mars one day.

The Moon Base plan: 3 phases

Announced by NASA in March 2026 – the three rooms of the game

🤖

Phase 1 · Build, Test, Learn

Robots, drones and uncrewed cargo landers scout the south pole and test the technology.

👣

Phase 2 · Back on the Moon

Astronauts land again, starting with Artemis IV, for short stays that rely on the supplies they bring.

🏠

Phase 3 · Long stays

Habitats, a pressurized rover and nuclear power let crews live at the base for months – a testing ground for Mars.

Mini space dictionary

 

🚀

SLS

The Space Launch System – NASA's giant rocket that launches the crew.

🛰️

Orion

The Artemis crew spacecraft. Astronauts fly in it and return to Earth in it.

🔗

Docking

Connecting two spacecraft in space – precisely and very slowly.

🌕

Lander

The spacecraft that takes astronauts from lunar orbit down to the surface and back up.

Discussion questions

After the game

  1. Why did NASA decide to practice docking near Earth before flying to the Moon? When else in life is it smart to "practice close to home"?
  2. Why is water on the Moon so important – even if nobody drinks it?
  3. There is no air on the Moon. What does that tell us about the dust in the crash movie? And about sound?
  4. Two crews, two companies, several countries: why are space missions built through cooperation today?
  5. If you were on the Artemis V crew, what would you want to build first at the Moon base?

All questions and answers

The correct answer is marked in green

Room 1: Phase 1 – Build, Test, Learn Phase 1 · Build, Test, Learn Code: 2026

1. What type of cargo is sent during Phase 1 of the Lunar Program?
Robots, drones, and unmanned cargo landers. · A crew of 12 astronauts to construct the initial living quarters. · An active nuclear reactor only, without any accompanying scientific equipment. · A crewed rover designed for long-term habitation.
Hint: Phase 1 prepares the ground – before people come to stay.
2. Which Artemis mission was the first crewed flight, carrying 4 astronauts around the Moon without landing on it?
Artemis II (April 2026). · Artemis I (November 2022). · Artemis III (2027). · Artemis VII (the 2030s).
Hint: It flew this year – and it wasn't the uncrewed test flight.
3. During the Artemis III mission, what key technological process will be tested in orbit around the Earth, in preparation for the crewed landing?
A docking demonstration between the Orion spacecraft and the Human Landing System (HLS). · Connecting a nuclear reactor to the "Lunar Cruiser" rover on the lunar surface. · Deploying the Multi-Purpose Habitat (MPH) at the South Pole. · Launching an uncrewed "Argonaut" lander back to Earth.
Hint: Two spacecraft need to connect safely in space.
4. Why did NASA divide the Artemis program into phases (robotic exploration – short crewed landings – permanent habitat) instead of building a permanent base right away?
To manage and reduce risk: each stage tests a different critical part (rocket safety – life support and docking – long-term survival and resource use) before the risk to human life grows. · Because NASA was waiting for other countries to develop all the necessary technologies on its behalf. · Because the Moon only changes its orbit around the Earth once every decade. · Because spacesuits were not available until the year 2030.
Hint: Think about safety: what if something goes wrong?

Room 2: Phase 2 – Back on the Moon Phase 2 · Crewed landings Code: 2028

5. What is unique about the landing site for the crewed Artemis missions (starting with Artemis IV)?
It is in the Moon's South Pole region, which has water ice in shadowed craters and areas that get nearly continuous sunlight. · It is on the Moon's equator, because of the comfortable temperatures. · It is on the far side ("the dark side") of the Moon, for shelter from radiation. · It is the exact historical landing site of Apollo 11.
Hint: Think about water – and about sunlight.
6. How is life support during a Phase 2 mission (such as Artemis IV) different from life support in Phase 3 (the Moon base)?
In Phase 2 the crew relies mainly on limited supplies carried by the lander for a short stay; Phase 3 needs closed-loop systems that recycle water and air for stays of months. · In Phase 2 no oxygen is needed at all, and in Phase 3 the crew breathes only nitrogen. · In Phase 2 astronauts are completely protected from radiation, and in Phase 3 there is no protection at all. · In Phase 3 astronauts can return to Earth within 5 minutes at any time.
Hint: A short visit vs. living there for months.
7. NASA wants to use local resources on the Moon (ISRU) – such as making oxygen and water from lunar ice. Why is this so important for a permanent home on the Moon and for future missions to Mars?
Because launching water, oxygen and fuel from Earth is very expensive and heavy; making them locally gives independence and greatly lowers mission costs. · Because water on the Moon is fresher and tastier than water on Earth. · Because bringing water from Earth is forbidden, due to the risk of contaminating the Moon. · Because the Orion spacecraft has no space at all for water bottles.
Hint: Everything launched from Earth costs a lot – because it's heavy.
8. NASA calls the Moon base a "testing ground" for Mars. What advantage does the Moon have as a testing ground, compared to flying straight to Mars?
The Moon is relatively close, so we can test habitats, robots and resource use while help or rescue is still possible in an emergency. · The atmospheres of the Moon and Mars are identical, so the flight is the same. · The Moon's gravity is stronger than both Earth's and Mars'. · The journey to Mars is shorter than the journey to the Moon.
Hint: How long does it take to reach the Moon? And Mars?

Room 3: Phase 3 – The Moon Base Phase 3 · Long stays Code: 2030

9. What is the permanent home planned for Phase 3 of the Moon base, and how long is it designed to last?
The Multi-Purpose Habitat (MPH), designed to last about 10 years. · The Lunar Gateway station, designed to land on the Moon for two years. · The Orion capsule, designed to hold 100 people for one year. · The Argonaut lander, only for emergency shelter for 24 hours.
Hint: Its name says it can be used for many purposes.
10. What is the special role of the JAXA/Toyota pressurized rover (the "Lunar Cruiser") in Phase 3?
To let astronauts live, work and travel in a protected, climate-controlled cabin – without spacesuits – for up to 30 days. · To launch rockets back to Earth on its own. · Only to dig water channels under the lunar surface. · To carry nuclear fuel straight to the International Space Station.
Hint: Think of a camper van – on the Moon.
11. Why does the permanent Moon base need both solar panels (on sunny peaks) and a nuclear reactor?
Because there is no sunlight inside shadowed craters or during the lunar night, and the reactor gives a steady supply of energy for heating and running the base. · Because solar panels only work when it rains on the Moon. · Because the nuclear reactor is mainly used to keep meteorites away from the base. · Because sunlight on the Moon is too weak to make any electricity.
Hint: What happens to solar panels at night?
12. Which surface vehicle is planned to launch on the Artemis VII mission in the 2030s, and what lets astronauts work in it without a spacesuit?
The Lunar Cruiser (by JAXA and Toyota) – a pressurized, climate-controlled vehicle where the crew can live and work for up to 30 days. · The Argonaut robotic cargo lander. · The Orion spacecraft. · The Gateway space station (habitation module).
Hint: You already met this vehicle in this room.
